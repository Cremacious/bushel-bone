# Bushel & Bone — Narrative & World Bible

**Status: IN PROGRESS.** Part 1 — The World is written (Session 3, GitHub issue #3). Parts 2–6 (Cast, Vane family, Season Arcs, sample events, voice guide) remain — tracked by issues #4, #5, #13, #14, #15, #16.

This bible fleshes out the story, the world, the NPCs, and the tone of Bushel & Bone in the depth required to actually write dialogue, event cards, and quest arcs without holes.

**Three foundational truths locked this session (issue #3):**
- **The Reckoning is real and the land keeps the ledger.** Under the Sull runs *the Marrow* — an old, pale seam that remembers the wronged dead and, when the account tips, returns them by name. Not a god; a reckoning that balances itself. (Player may never be told outright; we hold the truth.)
- **Whether making vessels is itself a sin stays an unsettled doctrinal dispute.** The Church of the Long Vigil preaches the Vat is blasphemy; Old Nan says the land only counts cruelty. Old Nan is closer to true — but not wholly right (see the Vat's faint stain, §6 drip), and no one alive can prove it.
- **The setting is an invented frontier nation** (the Commonwealth of Ostrey; the western territory called the Sull), so we invent freely and sidestep the D-018 cultural-care trap — the land's grievance belongs to the *ground*, not to any displaced people.

## PART 1 — THE WORLD  *(written — issue #3)*

*Naming note: everything below is invented. No real nation, faith, or people is depicted or thinly veiled (D-002, D-018). The Sull is genuinely unpeopled wildland — the only prior claim on it belongs to the ground itself, which is the whole point.*

### 1.1 The Setting Bible

**The nation — the Commonwealth of Ostrey.** A young charter-republic, roughly a century and a half old, in the industrial back-half of an alternate 1800s. Its eastern cities run on coal, chemistry, patent-law, and quiet money; its frontier runs on land-grants and nerve. Ostrey governs by *charter*: settlement companies buy the right to open new territory, sell parcels to homesteaders, and hold the mortgages. The state is thin on the frontier — circuit judges, a scatter of sheriffs, the charter companies' agents, and the churches.

**The frontier — the Sull.** The western territory where the game is set: low, wet, pale country of sedge-flats, cottonwood breaks, and slow rivers, not yet a full state. The Sull is *quick ground* — the soil is soft and warm in patches even in winter, unnaturally fertile in places, and the old-timers say bones don't stay put in it. Charter-settlement opened the Sull about a generation ago; it filled slowly, because the ground has a reputation. Marrow's Cross is the largest town for two days' ride.

**Nearby places** (texture for events and arcs): **Gallow's Ford** and **Tamsin's Reach** (rival Sull settlements), **Kettle Bottom** (a sinking, played-out farm town), **the Sinks** (marsh nobody homesteads). East, off the Sull: **Dray Junction** (railhead), then the market city of **Ledbury**, then the capital **Corvantine** (where vesselry is taught and the money lives).

**Time.** Kept by the Charter calendar; the game's "now" is the frontier's second generation — the founders are dying off, the Rail is coming, and living memory of the town's first winter is nearly gone. Precise Earth-year is deliberately unfixed ("alt-1880s"); in-world, characters date things by Charter years and by *"before/after the Salting"* (see §1.5).

**Currency.** The **mark**. Coppers are "irons," the coin generally "hard money." (Mechanically: the balance model's `coin` = marks.)

**The wider vessel-trade.** Vesselry — growing grown human bodies (§1.4) — is a *known, legal, faintly disreputable* eastern industry, like the patent-medicine or early-chemical trades: real, expanding, morally uneasy, loosely regulated. In Corvantine it's factory work (vessel-mills). On the frontier it's a wagon trade (merchants like Ambrose Vane) and, illegally-but-tolerated, home Vats. The supernatural undertow — the marrow-seams, the "come-backs" — is treated by the civilized world as Sull superstition, ghost stories for people who don't know better. Only those who live on the quick ground know it isn't. **Grounded Weird West: the horror is real but locally concentrated and widely disbelieved.**

**The Rail.** A charter-and-syndicate line pushing west from Ledbury through Dray Junction, reaching Marrow's Cross in the Year-3 arc ("The Rail Comes," §13). It ties the Sull's cheap grain, cheap vessels, and cheaper land to the eastern markets — prosperity and exposure in one iron thread. (Mechanically: unlocks the Rail Depot venue, §2.)

### 1.2 The Cosmology of the Marrow — what the Reckoning actually is

**The truth we hold (the player may never be told):** Beneath the Sull runs **the Marrow** — a pale seam in the earth, older than the nation, neither quite mineral nor quite flesh. It is not a god and does not think as we do. It *keeps an account.* The living draw from it (the quick ground's fertility); the wronged dead are paid into it. When the account tips too far out of balance, it settles the debt — it returns what was put in wrongly, by name, to the ground that knew them.

- **The currency of the ledger is the wronged dead** — those killed cruelly, worked to death, buried unmarked, denied rite. They are not destroyed. They are taken in, *remembered,* and returned when the account is called.
- **Cruelty is a debt. Rite is payment.** Funerals, blessings, the naming of the dead, the Vigil itself — these are how a debt is forgiven (mechanically: Reckoning atonement, §6). The land does not hate. It *collects.*
- **The five Reckoning tiers (§6), cosmologically:** *Whispers* — the seam stirs, and the ground remembers you are there. *Warnings* — it begins to call small debts (souring, blight, night-terrors). *Walkers* — it returns the named dead to their fields. *The Long Vigil Fails* — the rite that caps the Old Well weakens and the seam opens. *The Reckoning Proper* — the account is called in full, and the land takes the farm back into itself (land-loss by curse, D-007).

**The unsettled question (the doctrinal dispute — locked this session):** The Marrow registers a *faint* disquiet at vessels themselves — life grown from its own seam, that the land did not make. This is small (the §6 baseline drip; the Vat's standing drip) but real. So:

- **The Church of the Long Vigil** reads that faint stain as proof the Vat is blasphemy — *"quickening what the earth would not."* They are **exaggerating a true thing into doctrine.**
- **Old Nan** says the land only truly counts *blood laid down wrong* — cruelty and unrite — and that the Church's horror of the Vat is superstition dressed as piety. She is **closer to the truth, and understates the Vat's small stain.**
- **Neither is wholly right, and no one alive can prove it,** because the seam does not explain itself. This is the ambiguity by design (issue #3, Q2). It gives the moral system its argument: is a kind Vat-farmer innocent (Nan) or damned-but-slowly (Church)? The game never fully answers.

### 1.3 The two doctrines

**The Church of the Long Vigil** — an invented frontier faith (grim, watchful, home-grown; it rhymes with a hard Protestant register as *flavor,* depicts no real denomination). Founded in Marrow's Cross's first winter to *keep the marrow sleeping.*
- **Core creed:** the world is old and hungry underneath, and the living owe a debt of *vigilance.* To keep the Vigil is to watch, to burn light against the dark, and above all to *name the dead* so they rest and are not taken in.
- **The great rite — the Long Vigil:** the whole town watches through the longest night of Midwinter to renew the cap on the Old Well (the Midwinter festival, §12; "The Vigil Breaks," §13). Its clergy keep *the Watch* year-round.
- **Practices with mechanical weight:** the naming of the dead (funerals ease Reckoning, §6), the blessing of fields (§1/§12), the Vigil watches. Hardline preachers name vessels *"husks"* and their making heresy; **Preacher Elias Grange is a moderate** who baptizes vessels and buries them named — a standing tension the town resents and his arc turns on.

**Old Nan's counter-doctrine** — the older folk-belief that predates the Church, kept alive by one woman the Church calls a witch.
- **Core belief:** the land is not evil and owes nobody vigilance for merely *living* on it. What the marrow counts is *cruelty* — the unmarked grave, the worked-to-death, the betrayal. Grow your vessels and treat them gentle and the ground stays quiet; be cruel and no candle-watch will save you.
- She keeps the old rites the Church calls witchery — *folk-cleansing, reading the land, the quiet word over quick ground* (Old Nan's Reckoning intel and folk-cleansing, §1/§6). She is the game's truest oracle, which is exactly why the Church fears her.

### 1.4 Vesselry — the origin of the clones

**The work.** *Vesselry* (the trade), practiced by **vesselwrights** ("quickeners," colloquially). A grown human body is raised in weeks in a **Vat** — a tank of **vat-liquor** (a bath of quicklye and reagents) seeded with a fragment of living tissue. The product is a **vessel** (polite), a **clone** (technical), or a **husk** (cruel). Vessels are grown adult, blank-ish, and trainable — the labor of the game.

**The discovery (~40 years before the game).** A Corvantine physician-alchemist, **Doctor Sabine Orrell**, found that her process raised a true body — but only reliably over *quick ground.* The founders of vesselry never learned *why* it works, only *where:* near a marrow-seam it grows fast and true; far from one it fails, or comes out wrong. Orrell's students spread the trade east-to-west, chasing seams. **This is why the vessel-trade came to the Sull, and why Marrow's Cross is a node** — the quickest ground in the territory sits under the town.

**The buried link (the crux of the doctrinal dispute).** Vessels are, in a plain sense, grown *from the same stuff the land uses to remember.* The Church seizes on this — *the Vat feeds on the marrow, therefore it is unholy.* Old Nan shrugs — *the seam doesn't care what you grow in it, only what you kill in it.* Both are looking at the same true fact and reading it opposite. Vesselwrights themselves mostly don't think about it; it's a trade.

**Ambrose Vane** trained in Corvantine under Orrell's second-generation students, then came west to the Sull for the quick ground (cheaper, faster vessels) and the thin law. (His full biography, and his sister Meredith's, are Part 3 / issue #14.)

**Schools of thought** (for flavor and NPC argument): the **Millwrights** (eastern, industrial — vessels are product, grow them by the hundred), the **Gardeners** (vessels are husbandry — grow few, grow well), and the **Abstinents** (the Church line — grow none). Ambrose is a lapsed Millwright playing frontier Gardener; the Vat path (§3) is the player flirting with Millwright industry.

### 1.5 The History of Marrow's Cross

**Why it's called that — and the lie in the name.** The charter deed credits a surveyor, **Josiah Marrow,** who is said to have planted the boundary cross that named the town. The folk story says there was never any Josiah Marrow — that the charter-clerks invented a respectable man to write on the deed, because no one wanted to record what the crossroads was really named for: the two burial-tracks that met there, and the *marrow* underneath, and the cross the first settlers set over it to keep it down. (The truth: closer to the folk story. A good early-game rumor thread.)

**The founding and the Salting (the town's original sin — and the template for the whole game).** The first charter-party wintered at the crossroads about a generation ago and starved. To save the stores, the strongest **turned out the weakest** — the sick, the old, two children — to die in the cold, and buried them unmarked in the soft warm ground because it was the only ground that would dig in the frost. **That spring, the buried came back** — by name, to the doors they'd been turned from. The survivors' preacher put them down with a rite of watching and naming that lasted till dawn, and called it *the Long Vigil.* The town has kept the Vigil every Midwinter since. **Everything the player does with a clone and a shovel is a small echo of the Salting** — the game's moral engine is this founding, run again and again.

**The Old Well** (late-game unlock, §13 Year 7). The deepest shaft the founders sank into the marrow, looking for water and finding the seam. They capped it, set the first Vigil over the cap, and forbade its reopening. It is the town's throat to the thing underneath — where the Reckoning is closest to the surface, where the Vigil is *actually* kept, and where a late lineage may go looking for answers it would be wiser to leave buried.

### 1.7 Canon guardrails & how this maps to mechanics
